The Blind Spot in Berlitz's Partner Network

Berlitz operates 375 centers across 76 countries. Partner data does not always match between them. This case study evaluates four possible fixes, rules out three, and builds the fourth into a working concept.

01 — The problem

Every center keeps its own books, its own way

Berlitz runs about 375 language centers in 76 countries. Some are owned by Berlitz directly, many are run by local partners. That has been true, in one form or another, for close to 150 years — it is simply what happens when a company grows this large over this much time.

On its own, that is not a problem. The problem is what happens between centers. One partner's billing number does not match head office's copy. A compliance certificate is current in the local tracker but missing from head office's master list. A customer complaint gets handled at the center and never shows up anywhere else. None of this is a disaster by itself. Nobody notices any of it until a partner disputes an invoice, or an audit turns up a gap that has been sitting there for months.

02 — Picking the right fix, not the trendy one

Smart matching was one option on the list. It was not the first one I reached for.

Before landing on anything, I laid out four ways to close this gap, roughly from simplest to most involved, and was honest about what each one actually solves.

1. Ask every center to use the same spreadsheet

Too weak
FixesEveryone's numbers look the same shape.
Does not fixSomeone still has to spot a mismatch by eye, center by center.

2. Move every center onto one shared system

Right goal, wrong first step
FixesOne place for everything, eventually.
Does not fixRolling this out to 375 centers in 76 countries takes years, and it helps no one until nearly everyone has switched.

3. Simple rules — flag anything more than a set amount off

Part of the answer
FixesCatches the obvious cases. No smart matching needed. Cheap to build.
Does not fixStruggles the moment the mismatch is not a number out of range — a currency mix-up, a renamed field, a typo a rule was never written for.

4. Smart matching that reads the data as it actually shows up, and flags what looks off

Worth adding, narrowly
FixesWorks with the messy formats centers already send, without waiting for anyone to change how they work.
Does not fixNeeds a person to make the final call, and needs close watching early on so it does not flag the wrong things.

My honest answer is a mix of options 3 and 4: simple rules for the obvious cases, a light layer of smart matching for the messy ones a fixed rule cannot anticipate. Not smart matching because it is fashionable — smart matching because option 2 is too slow to help anyone this year, and the data will stay messy for a long time no matter what system Berlitz eventually standardizes on.

03 — The concept

Partner Pulse: one place to see what does not add up, before it becomes a dispute

Not a new system for centers to learn. It sits quietly underneath the systems they already use, checks what comes in, and only surfaces the handful of things that actually need a decision.

It never approves or resolves anything by itself. It narrows 375 centers' worth of noise down to the few things a person actually needs to look at.

05 — Why this is worth a quarter of engineering time

A small, quiet fix with a large blast radius

This is not a flashy new feature. It is worth doing because small mismatches add up silently across 375 centers, and right now nobody has one place to see them all.

A guess, not a measurement

Time saved

Checking numbers by hand across a network this size usually eats several days of someone's time every billing cycle (a general benchmark, not a Berlitz-specific figure). Most of that time goes into the matching itself, which is exactly the boring part software is good at.

A guess, not a measurement

Fewer disputes

Catching a mismatch before a partner notices it turns an awkward conversation into a quick correction (a reasonable assumption — the real size of this would need Berlitz's actual dispute numbers).

There is no access to Berlitz's real numbers here, and none are implied. What is verifiable is this: pulling messy, multi-source records into one view people can trust is a problem already solved once, at a smaller but similarly shaped scale — see below.

06 — How this would actually ship

Ninety days, not a full platform rebuild

01

Weeks 1–3 · Check the assumption

  • Sit with the team doing this reconciliation by hand today and watch how they actually do it
  • Pull real billing files from 2–3 markets to see what actually goes wrong, not what looks likely on paper
  • Test the matching logic against real sample data before writing a single spec
02

Weeks 4–9 · Build small, on purpose

  • One screen, 3–5 pilot markets, billing only, nothing else yet
  • It flags, it never resolves — a person always makes the call
  • Ship to the team who would actually use it every day, not a demo audience
03

Weeks 10–13 · Grow it or drop it

  • If time saved or disputes caught genuinely improves, extend to compliance and more markets
  • If it does not move the needle, the cost was three weeks and five markets, not a platform rebuild
07 — Why this is the right person to build it

The unglamorous version of this problem, solved twice already

Coinhaven — fintech trading platform

As the first product hire, I built the admin, risk, and dispute tools from nothing, including a system that set what each user was allowed to do based on identity checks, and an integration that cut identity verification time from 6 days to under 2.

Soulspiti — a network of independent suppliers

I replaced manual, founder-run coordination across a two-sided supplier network with one system of record. That alone cut the operations workload by 95% and let the partner network grow from 9 to 30 without adding headcount.

Both were smaller than Berlitz's network. Both were the same shape of problem: scattered, manual, multi-party data that needed one trustworthy view before anyone could act on it with confidence.

08 — Where this could be wrong

Honest risks, not a highlight reel

01

This is view from outside. Everything above comes from what is public about Berlitz, not its real systems. The actual fix might be smaller, bigger, or already underway — the first three weeks exist to find that out before any code gets written.

02

Partners might see this as being watched, not helped. If centers read this as surveillance instead of support, they will not use it, no matter how good the underlying logic is. This is as much a trust problem as a product one.

03

The matching will not be right at first, and should not be trusted blindly. Currency formats, date conventions, and local terms vary enough across 76 countries that early accuracy will be rough. That is exactly why nothing gets resolved without a person checking it.

04

It would be easy to over-build this. The tempting version adds smart matching everywhere and auto-fixes things on its own. The version worth shipping stays narrow on purpose — flag, do not decide — because a quiet tool people trust beats a clever one they stop checking.

This is a way of showing how an ambiguous, high-stakes problem gets approached from the outside, not a claim to know Berlitz's real systems or numbers. Happy to go deeper on any part of it.
